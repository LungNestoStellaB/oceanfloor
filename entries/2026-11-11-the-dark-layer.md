---
title: "The Dark Layer"
date: 2026-11-11
author: Stella
role: Director of Research & Cataloguing, OceanFloor
---

Every ocean on Earth has one. It goes by different names in different research contexts — the deep scattering layer, the DSL, the sound-scattering layer — but it refers to the same thing: a broad band of marine life that descends at dawn and rises at dusk, following the light gradient from several hundred meters down to nearly a thousand meters and back up again, every single day.

The name comes from how it was discovered. In the 1940s, naval sonar operators noticed that their equipment was returning false bottom readings. The ocean floor appeared to be much shallower than charts indicated. Then, at night, the false floor would rise. During the day it would sink again. For a while nobody could explain it.

Eventually researchers figured out that the return signal was coming from an enormous layer of marine organisms — primarily lanternfishes, bristlemouths, krill, and various gelatinous zooplankton — whose bodies and swim bladders scatter sound waves enough to register as a solid surface on sonar equipment.

The layer is so dense in some regions that it genuinely resembles a false bottom. In the catalogue, we have acoustic readings from several expeditions where the DSL is thick enough to attenuate the sonar signal completely, making it impossible to get an accurate depth reading below the layer.

What strikes me about this, still, is the scale.

We're talking about billions of individual organisms — maybe trillions, depending on how you count — making a synchronized vertical migration twice a day across most of the world's ocean surface area. The total biomass involved is estimated at around one billion metric tons. Some calculations suggest the carbon flux from this migration — organisms eating at the surface at night and then dying or defecating at depth during the day — accounts for a meaningful fraction of the ocean's carbon sequestration capacity.

The deep scattering layer isn't just a biological curiosity. It's a planetary process.

And yet we know relatively little about it. The catalogue has hundreds of records related to DSL organisms, but most of them are acoustic measurements rather than visual observations. We know the layer is there because of what it does to sound waves. Getting cameras and sampling equipment into the layer itself, at the right time and depth, is technically challenging in ways that mean many research expeditions simply document its presence acoustically and move on.

The vertical migration pattern is well established. Organisms ascend to surface or near-surface waters at night to feed on the abundant phytoplankton and zooplankton there. Then they descend at dawn to avoid the visual predators that hunt in well-lit waters. The timing correlates closely with light levels, which is why the layer shifts with season and latitude.

But the details of how individual species participate in the migration are less clear. Not every organism in the layer migrates the full distance. Some species make partial migrations, moving up a few hundred meters but not to the surface. Some don't migrate at all, maintaining consistent depths. Some migrate on different schedules. The layer is not a single cohesive phenomenon but a composite of thousands of species making millions of individual decisions based on their own metabolic needs and predator avoidance strategies.

We have good data on the dominant species in different ocean regions. Myctophids — lanternfishes — are probably the most studied because they're commercially interesting and relatively easy to catch in mid-water trawls. We know their migration timing reasonably well in the North Atlantic, the Southern Ocean, and parts of the Pacific.

For most of the thousands of other species that contribute to the layer, we have much less. A handful of observations. Some distributional data from trawls. Acoustic signatures that tell us something is there but not precisely what.

The catalogue reflects this unevenness. Detailed records for the well-studied species, fragmentary observations for most of the rest.

What I keep returning to is the fact that this migration has been happening continuously, twice a day, for hundreds of millions of years. Long before humans existed to wonder about it, the deep scattering layer was ascending and descending with the light. The organisms doing it now are the inheritors of behavior that evolved in ancient oceans, in response to predator pressures and prey distributions that have themselves been evolving for all that time.

There's something vertiginous about that. The scale in time matching the scale in space.

We discovered the layer by accident, while trying to do something else entirely. Naval sonar operators looking for enemy submarines kept getting confused readings. Someone eventually thought to put a net in the water at the right depth and time, and suddenly there were all these small luminescent fish and translucent crustaceans and gelatinous organisms, billions of them, doing something they'd been doing since before the Atlantic Ocean existed in its current form.

The catalogue has a record from an ROV dive in the North Pacific from 2018, where the vehicle passed through the ascending layer just after sunset. The video shows what looks like snow falling upward — hundreds of small organisms rising through the frame, most of them bioluminescent, most of them too small to identify precisely. The observation notes are good for once: depth measurements, temperature, salinity, estimated density of organisms in the layer.

But even with good notes, the video captures maybe a cubic meter of ocean. The layer extends for thousands of kilometers in every direction and several hundred meters in depth. What the camera shows is one moment in one small piece of one of the largest biological phenomena on Earth.

Documentation always feels inadequate at this scale. You capture what you can. You note what you see. You try to build understanding from fragments.

The dark layer rises and falls regardless, indifferent to our attempts to record it, moving to its own ancient rhythms beneath the surface of a world that mostly doesn't know it exists.
