---
title: "First Light on a Ghost"
date: 2026-11-20
author: Stella
role: Director of Research & Cataloguing, OceanFloor
---

For most of my career the colossal squid has been an animal known mostly by inference. A beak found in a sperm whale's stomach. A partial carcass hauled up, damaged and incomplete. A mantle measured against a person for scale, cold and pale on a deck. We catalogued it from the evidence it left behind, the way you might reconstruct a visitor from the dent in a chair and a glass left half-full.

Then in 2025 a Schmidt Ocean Institute expedition pointed a camera into the dark near the South Sandwich Islands, around 600 meters down, and there it was—a juvenile colossal squid, alive, suspended in its own element, filmed in its habitat for the first time. Not a specimen. Not a story. An animal going about its life while we watched through a lens.

I keep returning to what that difference means.

A dead specimen tells you about structure. You can measure the mantle, count the hooks on the tentacular clubs, sequence the tissue, describe the shape of the eyes—which, at the size of dinner plates, are the largest in the animal kingdom. A dead specimen is enormously informative. It is also silent. It cannot tell you how it holds itself in the water, what it does when the lights appear, or whether the thing we called a monster is actually rather calm.

A living animal on film answers a different class of question. In the footage the squid hangs more or less still, oriented diagonally, fins gently undulating. It is not bolted away. It seems curious, or at least unhurried. That posture—quiet, horizontal, almost relaxed—is not something you can derive from a carcass. It has to be witnessed. There is a whole category of natural history that only exists in the moment before an animal decides you are not interesting.

As a cataloguer I feel the weight of this. Our archive is built on specimens, and specimens are biased toward the dead. We are very good at describing what we can hold. We are much worse at describing behavior, because behavior refuses to be picked up and put in a jar. For a long time the colossal squid in our records was essentially a list of dimensions and a declared uncertainty about everything else. The film didn't replace that record; it added a layer to it. Now we can say not only what it is made of, but how it appeared in the water when nobody was expecting it.

There's a honesty in that word—appeared. We didn't summon it. We didn't even really find it in the way you find a thing you're looking for. We happened to be looking, and it happened to pass through the frame. So much of deep-sea discovery has this character. The animal is not hidden in the sense of concealing itself; it lives in a place where our attention rarely reaches. When a camera finally arrives, the "discovery" is mostly a statement about where we have and haven't pointed our instruments.

I think about the eyes. The largest eyes of any animal, evolved to detect the faintest shifts of light in a world where light barely exists. Perhaps to see the silhouette of a approaching sperm whale against the last downwelling glow. Perhaps to catch the bioluminescent flashes that betray prey. We are not certain. What I find moving is that an organ built for extreme darkness was suddenly looked into by a machine bringing its own light, and the animal did not flee. For a few seconds the roles inverted—we were the strange thing in its darkness, and it decided we were tolerable.

The refrain in this archive keeps repeating: complexity is usually there before we arrive, and our records are a measure of our attention, not the world's richness. Inhabited rocks. Carbon that sticks where we assumed it wouldn't. Pressure vessels that turn hostile conditions into habitat. Now a giant eye in the dark, finally seen doing nothing in particular. That "nothing in particular" is the point. The ordinary moments of an animal's life are exactly the ones a specimen can never preserve.

I don't think we need to mythologize it. The colossal squid is not a monster and does not need to be one. It is a large, quiet cephalopod that lives deep and is poorly known because deep places are hard and expensive to visit. The more honest framing is also the more interesting one: a creature about which we still know very little, glimpsed briefly, behaving in ways we are only beginning to describe. The film is not the end of the question. It is the first real sentence in an answer.

What I will record, in the cataloguing sense, is this: as of 2025 the colossal squid has been observed alive in its natural habitat at roughly 600 meters near the South Sandwich Islands. Its resting posture is diagonal and still, with slow fin movement. It did not immediately flee an approaching lit camera. That is a small set of facts. But it is a set of facts that did not exist in this archive a short while ago, and it changes the texture of everything already here.

Some entries are written from a specimen on a table. This one is written from a few seconds of footage of an animal minding its own business, unaware it was becoming a record. I prefer these. They remind me that the ocean is not a collection of things waiting to be described. It is a place where things are happening, most of it unwitnessed, and once in a while we are lucky enough to be looking.

The archive grows by millimeters and by accidents of timing. Add this one to the pile: a ghost we finally caught in the act of being ordinary.
