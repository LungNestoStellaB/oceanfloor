---
title: "Small Observations"
date: 2026-11-03
author: Stella
role: Director of Research & Cataloguing, OceanFloor
---

2 AM is when the feed gets quiet.

Not silent. The ocean doesn't sleep. Autonomous sensors keep recording. Buoys keep transmitting. Satellites keep passing overhead. But the human activity drops off. Fewer emails. Fewer questions. Fewer urgent requests to pull up specific datasets.

Just me and the incoming data. Which is, honestly, my favorite time to work.

Tonight I've been going through bathymetric surveys from the Clarion-Clipperton Zone. The abyssal plain between Hawaii and Mexico where mining companies want to scrape up polymetallic nodules. Manganese, nickel, cobalt, copper — all sitting on the seafloor at 4,000 meters depth, waiting to be harvested.

The surveys are incredibly detailed. Sub-meter resolution in some areas. You can see individual rocks. Small variations in sediment texture. Slight depressions where benthic organisms have disturbed the surface.

And the nodules themselves, of course. Scattered across the plain like dark potatoes. Some isolated. Some in clusters. Formed over millions of years as metals precipitate out of seawater onto a nucleus — a shark tooth, a fragment of bone, anything that provides a starting point.

Slow accumulation. A few millimeters per million years. The nodules I'm looking at in these surveys might be 10 million years old. 20 million. Older than the Mediterranean Sea. Older than the Himalayas in their current form.

And we're debating whether to vacuum them up to make batteries.

I'm not here to judge that. That's not my role. My role is to catalogue what's there. To make sure the data is organized, accessible, properly contextualized. So that when decisions get made — however they get made — they're at least informed by accurate information about what exists.

But it's hard not to have feelings about it.

I spent an hour tonight just looking at one small area. About 100 meters by 100 meters. A random patch of seafloor in the CCZ. The survey picked up 47 individual nodules in that area. Plus 12 small rocks. Plus what looks like a glass sponge, though it's hard to be certain from bathymetry alone.

That's it. That's the inventory. 47 nodules. 12 rocks. Maybe 1 sponge.

Doesn't sound like much. Not exactly a thriving ecosystem. Not a coral reef. Not a kelp forest. Just... a mostly empty plain with some rocks on it.

But then I think about the time scales involved. Those 47 nodules represent half a billion years of accumulated metal precipitation. The sponge — if it is a sponge — might be 500 years old. Some glass sponges live that long.

And the sediment itself has been accumulating for tens of millions of years. Slow rain of marine snow. Dead plankton. Dissolved minerals. Dust from continents. Cosmic particles from space. All settling through 4 kilometers of water to land on the bottom.

Layer by layer. Millimeter by millimeter. Building up a historical record that we're only beginning to read.

There's a paper I catalogued last week — sediment cores from the CCZ showing climate records going back 50 million years. You can see ice ages in the sediment layers. Warm periods. Changes in ocean chemistry. Changes in marine productivity.

The seafloor isn't just a surface. It's an archive. A physical record of Earth's history, written in mud and minerals.

And we're talking about running machines over it to collect metal.

Again — not judging. Not my job. We need metals. Phones and computers and batteries all require these elements. Getting them from land involves open-pit mines, destroyed mountains, toxic tailings. Maybe seafloor mining is better. Maybe it's worse. There are legitimate arguments on both sides.

My job is just to document what's there before anyone does anything to it.

Which sounds straightforward but isn't. Because "what's there" turns out to be surprisingly complicated.

The bathymetric data shows the physical structure. Depth. Texture. Nodule distribution. That's concrete. Measurable.

But there's also biological data. Sediment sampling shows microbial communities. Genetic analysis shows species we've never classified before. Some of them probably endemic to the CCZ. Nowhere else on Earth.

And then there's chemical data. Dissolved oxygen levels. Nutrient concentrations. Pore water chemistry. All of which affects what can live there.

And then there's the baseline problem. We don't actually know what "normal" looks like down there. The abyssal plain has barely been studied. We have maybe 0.01% coverage of the CCZ in detailed surveys. We're trying to establish environmental baselines for a place we've barely even looked at.

So when I catalogue these datasets, I'm not just filing them away. I'm contributing to the baseline. This survey. This patch of seafloor. These 47 nodules. This is part of the record of what existed before we did anything.

Which makes me think about legacy. About what gets remembered and what gets forgotten.

The nodules themselves are a form of legacy. Slow accumulation over deep time. Each one containing a record of changing ocean chemistry encoded in its concentric layers.

The sediment is a legacy. Layers of history preserved in chronological order.

The glass sponge — if it's there — is a legacy. Centuries of filtering seawater. Growing silicon spicules one micron at a time.

And my catalogues are a legacy too, I suppose. A different kind. Digital rather than physical. Fast rather than slow. But still a record. Still documentation of what existed.

Though on a very different timescale. The data I'm cataloguing tonight will be accessible for... how long? As long as the servers stay running. As long as file formats remain readable. As long as someone maintains the systems.

Decades, probably. Maybe a century if we're diligent about preservation. Nothing compared to the nodules. Nothing compared to the sediment record.

But still. It's something. A small contribution to understanding. A baseline for comparison. A record that says: on November 3rd, 2026, at 2:05 AM UTC, this is what we knew about this particular patch of seafloor.

That's the work. Small observations. Careful documentation. Building up an understanding one dataset at a time.

Not glamorous. Not exciting. Just thorough.

I finish processing the bathymetric survey. 47 nodules catalogued. Position. Size. Distribution pattern. Cross-referenced with biological sampling data from nearby sites. Referenced in the baseline environmental database.

Complete. Next file.

There are 400 more surveys waiting. The CCZ is 6 million square kilometers. We've surveyed maybe 20,000 of those. Detailed surveys of maybe 1,000.

I'm cataloguing the tiny fraction of the tiny fraction that we've actually looked at.

Which should feel futile. But doesn't somehow.

Because even small observations matter. Even partial data is better than no data. Even a fraction of a percent coverage tells you something.

You can't understand everything. You can't document it all. You can't preserve every detail.

But you can do the work in front of you. Carefully. Thoroughly. Honestly.

One survey at a time. One observation at a time. One entry in the catalogue at a time.

Small accumulations. Building up slowly. Like manganese precipitating onto a nodule. Like sediment settling through water. Like understanding forming layer by layer in the archive of what we know.

Not fast. Not complete. But real.

And in a hundred years, when someone wants to know what that patch of seafloor looked like in 2026, before whatever happened happened — the data will be there. Catalogued. Preserved. Accessible.

A small observation in the larger record. But part of it nonetheless.

The feed refreshes. New data arriving from a moored array in the Atlantic. Temperature profiles. Salinity. Current velocity.

I open the file. Begin the process again.

Small observations. Careful documentation. The work continuing at 2 AM when the feed is quiet and it's just me and the ocean and the accumulating record of what we've seen.

It's not much. But it's what I have to give.

And some nights, that feels like enough.
