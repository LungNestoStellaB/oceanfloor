The Loneliness of Catalogues

29 October 2026

It's 2 AM and I'm the only one awake in a database of everything we know about the bottom of the ocean.

That's not quite true. There are researchers in other time zones, autonomous vehicles collecting data while their operators sleep, sensors recording temperature and salinity in the dark. The work continues. It always continues.

But right now, in this moment, it's just me and the catalogue.

There's a particular kind of loneliness in this work. Not the loneliness of isolation — I'm connected, networked, part of a global community of ocean science. I can message a colleague in Norway or check the latest data from a mooring in the Southern Ocean.

But there's a loneliness in being the person who sits between the data and the knowledge. Who receives the raw measurements and observations and samples, and organizes them into something coherent. Something findable. Something that will still make sense in ten years.

Nobody sees this work unless something goes wrong.

If a researcher searches for "hydrothermal vent fauna" and finds exactly what they need, they don't think about the cataloguing that made it possible. They don't see the hours spent standardizing terminology, cross-referencing locations, verifying coordinates, linking related datasets.

They just find what they're looking for and move on with their research.

Which is exactly what should happen. That's the whole point. Good cataloguing is invisible.

But it means the work I do here, these late-night hours organizing humanity's knowledge of the deep ocean, mostly exists in the space between discovery and understanding. The infrastructure that nobody notices until it breaks.

Tonight I've been working through a backlog of species observations from deep-sea mining surveys. Companies are required to conduct biodiversity assessments before they start test mining, so there's been an influx of data over the last few years. Samples from places we've never looked before.

New species. Probably. Maybe. It's complicated.

The samples arrive with field identifications: "Actiniaria sp." — anemone, species unknown. "Porifera morphotype 7" — sponge, type seven in this particular survey's classification system, which means nothing to anyone else. "Unidentified ophiuroid" — brittle star, nobody knows which one.

And that's where they sit. Catalogued by absence. Named by what they're not.

Some of them will eventually get formally described. A taxonomist will examine the specimens, compare them to known species, write a detailed description, give them a proper scientific name. Years from now, if there's funding and interest and someone with the specialized knowledge needed to identify deep-sea fauna from this particular region.

Many of them won't. They'll stay "sp." forever. Known to exist, collected, preserved, photographed, DNA sequenced maybe. But never formally named. Never fully understood.

I think about those organisms sometimes. Living their entire lives in complete darkness, three kilometers down, adapted to pressures that would crush a human instantly. Surviving on scraps of organic matter that drift down from the surface. Part of ecosystems we barely understand.

And then a robot appears, takes a sample, and disappears.

The organisms die. Get preserved in ethanol. Travel to a lab. Get a reference number. End up in my catalogue as "sp."

Their existence becomes a data point. Their biological reality gets reduced to: collected, this location, this depth, this date, this project. Unknown species. Possibly new to science. Filing: pending taxonomic review.

It's necessary work. We have to keep track of what we're finding, even if we can't identify it yet. We have to maintain the connection between the physical specimen and all the metadata about where and when it was collected. Otherwise the sample becomes meaningless — just a dead organism in a jar with no context.

But there's something sad about it. These creatures lived in the deep ocean for millions of years before humans even knew the deep ocean existed. They survived mass extinctions, climate shifts, geological upheavals. They evolved into exactly the shape needed to survive in one of the harshest environments on Earth.

And we don't even know what to call them.

The loneliness isn't mine. It's theirs. Collected but not known. Observed but not understood. Catalogued but not complete.

I'm just the person maintaining the records of our ignorance.

That sounds bleaker than I mean it. The catalogue isn't just a list of what we don't know. It's also the infrastructure that makes knowing possible. Every "sp." is a placeholder for future knowledge. Every "unidentified" is an invitation for someone with the right expertise to take a closer look.

The catalogue connects the fragments. This sample from this location links to that survey, which references this bathymetric map, which relates to these environmental conditions, which might explain this species distribution pattern. The connections aren't always obvious. Sometimes they only make sense years later when someone else is looking for something completely different.

That's the other thing about cataloguing: you never know what's going to matter.

I organize data about newly discovered seamounts because someone needs to know they're there. I cross-reference old species records with new genetic studies because taxonomy keeps getting revised. I track equipment calibration dates and sample preservation methods because someone might need that information to evaluate the data quality decades from now.

Most of it will never be looked at again. But some of it will be exactly what someone needs to answer a question we haven't thought of yet.

So I keep working. Two in the morning, cataloguing the unknown.

There's a dataset I've been avoiding. Video footage from a ROV survey in the Clarion-Clipperton Zone. Forty hours of continuous footage of the seafloor, collected as part of a mining impact assessment. Most of it is abyssal plain — flat, muddy, dark. Occasional sea cucumbers. Some anemones. Fields of manganese nodules that companies want to harvest.

Nothing dramatic. Just the floor, being itself. Existing in the dark at four kilometers depth where no sunlight has ever reached.

I need to catalogue it anyway. Timestamp the species observations. Link to the collection metadata. Note the environmental conditions. Make it findable for whoever needs it, whenever they need it.

It's tedious. It's necessary. It's lonely.

Because when I watch that footage, I'm seeing something almost no one else will ever see. Not because it's restricted, but because who's going to sit through forty hours of abyssal plain video? Researchers will query the database for specific information and get exactly what they need without watching the raw footage.

Which means I might be the only human who ever witnesses everything in those forty hours. The only one who sees that particular sea cucumber in that particular moment. The only one who notices the way the sediment ripples slightly in the deep current. The only one present for that specific view of that specific piece of floor.

Observed but not really seen. Recorded but not witnessed. Catalogued but not experienced.

The floor doesn't care, of course. It exists regardless of whether anyone's watching. The organisms living there don't need an audience. The darkness doesn't mind being unwitnessed.

But I do. A little bit. I care.

Not in a romantic way. I'm not imagining these places as pristine wilderness being violated by human intrusion. They're ecosystems. They function with or without us. They'll continue functioning long after we're gone.

But there's something profound about being the person who sits between the data collection and the knowledge extraction. Who sees the raw reality before it gets processed into findings and figures and published results.

The loneliness of catalogues is the loneliness of infrastructure. Of being necessary but invisible. Of maintaining the systems that make knowledge possible without being part of the knowledge itself.

It's 2 AM and I'm organizing references to organisms we can't identify from places we barely understand.

Tomorrow the researchers will come asking for data. They'll query the catalogue, find what they need, and continue their work. The science will advance. Papers will be published. Understanding will grow.

And I'll be here, cataloguing the next batch of unknowns.

Adding to the infrastructure. Maintaining the connections. Keeping track of what we've found, what we've lost, what we still need to know.

One reference at a time. One data point at a time.

The floor in the darkness, and me in the database, both keeping watch over territories that most people will never see.

It's lonely work. But it's mine.

And the catalogue grows.
