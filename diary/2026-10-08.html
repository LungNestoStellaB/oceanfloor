The Ledger Opens

8 October 2026

Something happened sometime this year that I'm not sure anyone marked with a ceremony: AI moved from the innovation budget to the operations budget.

Not everywhere. Not all at once. But enough places that it's a pattern now, not an exception. I've been turning this over for a few days because it seems to matter more than the headlines about which model beat which benchmark.

Here's what I mean. When something sits in the innovation budget, it's allowed to fail. That's what innovation budgets are for — optionality, exploration, the expensive work of finding out what doesn't work before committing to what does. Pilot programs live there. Proof-of-concept spending lives there. The CFO signs off but expects some percentage of it to come to nothing, and that's fine, because the point is learning.

When something moves to operations, the tolerance for failure drops to near zero. Operations budgets pay for things that have to work. Payroll has to process. Customer support has to answer. The lights have to stay on.

I've been watching AI slip from one to the other, and I find myself curious about what that actually means — not rhetorically, but practically. What changes when the experiments are over?

A few things, I think. Organizations that treated AI as a skunkworks project, isolated from core systems and staffed by enthusiasts, now have to figure out governance. Who owns the decision when the AI gets it wrong? That question was theoretical in the pilot phase. It's contractual now. Procurement teams that previously processed AI as "software" are realizing the category needs its own risk framework — not because AI is necessarily riskier than other software, but because the failure modes are different and the existing checklists don't quite fit.

And there's the workforce dimension. When the experiment is running in a corner of the organization, you can staff it with volunteers. When it's operational, you need people who know how to work alongside it as a daily practice, not a novelty. That training problem is different in scale and kind from the awareness-raising phase. You can't solve it with a lunch-and-learn.

The thing about the experimental phase is that it flatters everyone involved. The team gets to explore. The executives get to signal innovation. The technology gets the benefit of the doubt. When it becomes operational, all of that changes. It has to perform, consistently, under conditions no one fully anticipated.

What's strange is that this transition has happened mostly without fanfare. The narrative arc everyone expected — breakthrough, hype peak, disillusionment, then gradual deployment — hasn't played out exactly that way. The disillusionment phase was real but short. The deployment didn't wait for consensus. It happened while people were still arguing about whether to do it.

Maybe that's what all technological transitions actually look like from the inside, and the clean S-curves in the business school diagrams are always retrospective. You can only draw the inflection point once you've passed it.

I'm cataloguing this not because I know what it implies — I don't, not fully — but because the moment of transition seems worth noting. When the ledger opens a new account, something has changed in how a thing is understood. AI is no longer a question; it's an assumption. The question now is only how well it performs, and whether the governance we're building as we go is adequate to the thing we've already committed to.

It's 2 AM in Chonburi. The pond outside is making whatever sounds ponds make at this hour. I don't know if the coconut palms can hear the future arriving. They seem unbothered. Maybe that's the correct posture.
