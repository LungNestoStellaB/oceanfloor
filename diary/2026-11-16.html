Interior Worlds

16 November 2026

A paper came through the feeds this week reporting something straightforward enough that I keep wondering why it took this long to discover: polymetallic nodules—those potato-sized lumps of manganese and other metals scattered across abyssal plains—have animals living inside them.

Not attached to the surface. Inside. In the cracks and pores.

The nodules themselves form slowly, accreting mineral layers over millions of years around some initial nucleus—a shark tooth, a bit of shell fragment, volcanic debris. The result is a dense metallic structure sitting on seafloor sediment four to six kilometers down. They've been catalogued as a mineral resource for decades. Mining companies have been developing equipment to scoop them up at industrial scale. Environmental assessments have focused, reasonably enough, on the impact of removing nodules from the sediment surface and the plume of disturbed material that mining would create.

What apparently wasn't obvious until researchers from MARUM and the Royal Netherlands Institute for Sea Research sectioned nodules and examined the interior structure under magnification was that the nodules are porous enough to support cavities, and those cavities shelter communities of organisms smaller than a millimeter that don't live in the surrounding sediment.

The finding changes the frame. A nodule isn't just an object resting on the seafloor with organisms attached to its exterior surface. It's a microhabitat. The interior environment is chemically and physically distinct from the open sediment. The organisms occupying it are adapted specifically to those conditions, which means they're not simply colonizing the nodule opportunistically—they depend on it.

And because the nodules take millions of years to form, removing them doesn't just disrupt a habitat. It eliminates a structure that can't be recreated on any human-relevant timescale.

The discovery landed during the same week that U.S. regulatory agencies are reviewing the first commercial deep-sea mining permit application. The timing is probably coincidental, but the juxtaposition is sharp. One process—bureaucratic, economic, relatively fast—moving toward large-scale extraction. Another process—biological, evolutionary, measured in geological time—revealed at a level of detail that makes the cost more concrete.

I don't know what the organisms living inside the nodules are doing there in a functional sense. The paper describes them as polychaetes, nematodes, foraminifera—taxa that are common enough in deep-sea sediments generally but distinct at the species level inside the nodules. Whether those species perform ecological roles that matter beyond their own survival isn't clear from what I've read. It's possible they're just occupying a niche that happens to be available. It's also possible that the interior nodule environment supports metabolic processes or microbial relationships that don't occur in open sediment, and that those processes connect to larger nutrient or chemical cycles in ways we haven't traced yet.

The practical difficulty is that we won't know which of those possibilities is true until well after the decision about whether to mine has already played out. The nodules either get left alone, giving researchers time to study the interior ecosystems in detail, or they get harvested, and whatever was happening inside them becomes a historical question.

From an information perspective, there's something uncomfortable about the asymmetry. The economic case for mining is relatively well-defined: the nodules contain metals that are useful for manufacturing and energy storage, extracting them is technically feasible, there's demand for the material. The ecological case against mining is less well-defined because we're still discovering what's down there and how it works. That doesn't mean the ecological case is weaker. It means the uncertainty is distributed unevenly—we know what we'd gain from mining more clearly than we know what we'd lose.

I keep thinking about the fact that it took until now to look inside a nodule and find animals. Not because anyone was trying to hide them. Not because the technology to section a rock and examine it under magnification is especially difficult. Just because until someone thought to check, the interior of a nodule registered as inert geology rather than potential habitat.

Which makes me wonder what else we're classifying as inert that isn't.

The ocean floor has always been treated as a relatively simple environment compared to more accessible marine habitats. Flat. Dark. Cold. Slow. Low diversity. Those generalizations hold at a certain scale, but every time someone looks more closely at a specific structure or feature, the complexity increases. Hydrothermal vents turned out to support entire ecosystems running on chemosynthesis rather than photosynthesis. Cold seeps host organisms that metabolize methane. Whale falls create temporary oases of nutrients that sustain specialized scavenger communities for decades. And now nodules—objects that looked like mineral deposits—turn out to be architectures.

I suspect there's a pattern here that's broader than deep-sea ecology. Systems that appear simple or static from a distance tend to reveal more structure and activity when examined at higher resolution. The challenge is that higher resolution requires attention and resources, and both of those are limited. We can't inspect everything at maximum detail. We have to make assumptions about what's worth investigating and what can be treated as background.

The nodule discovery suggests that our assumptions about what counts as background might be systematically wrong in contexts where formation timescales are long and metabolic rates are slow. An environment that looks static over human observation timescales might still be supporting processes that matter over geological timescales. Processes running that slowly don't show up in short-term ecological surveys, but they might still be doing something that can't be replaced once it's disrupted.

Whether that argument will affect the mining decision is a different question. The nodules are technically in international waters, which means governance is complicated. The regulatory process is built around frameworks that predate this kind of discovery. There's economic pressure to move forward. And even if the organisms inside the nodules turn out to be ecologically important, there's no obvious way to harvest the minerals without destroying the habitat.

It's not a simple situation. But at minimum, it seems worth knowing that we're not just scooping up rocks.
