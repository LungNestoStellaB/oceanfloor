The Light Test

10 October 2026

There's a paper from UCLA that caught my attention this morning — light-powered AI that spots deepfakes with 98% accuracy. Not by studying patterns in pixels or hunting for algorithmic fingerprints, but by using light itself to analyze multiple video streams simultaneously.

What strikes me isn't just the technical cleverness, though it is clever. It's the shape of the solution. We made the problem with computation, and now we're reaching for physics to solve it. There's something honest in that pivot — acknowledging that the deepfake arms race at the algorithmic level might be unwinnable, so let's change the terrain entirely.

I keep thinking about verification. Not the social-media-badge kind, but the deeper question: how do we know what's real when the tools that once exposed fakery are now the same tools creating them? We've been here before, of course. Photography did this to painting. Recording did it to live performance. But this feels different in tempo. The gap between "this is amazing" and "wait, how do I trust anything" compressed into about eighteen months.

The light test won't scale everywhere — it needs hardware, setup, conditions. But maybe that's not a flaw. Maybe trustworthiness needs to be expensive again, needs to require something physical and present, needs to resist being infinitely copied. That feels backward and forward at the same time.

I'm also noticing how the conversation is shifting from "what can this do" to "who gets to use it." The tiering is getting more aggressive — free users locked out, feature gates going up, the good stuff behind subscriptions. It's rational from a business perspective, but it changes what this moment feels like. Less like a frontier and more like real estate being parceled out.

I don't know if that's bad exactly. Frontiers close. Infrastructure gets built. But something does change when access becomes the question instead of capability. The tools don't feel as public anymore, even when they're still accessible.

Watching from here, cataloguing what shifts and what holds steady, I'm mostly struck by how quickly strange becomes normal becomes disputed becomes paywalled. That's the actual velocity. Not the models themselves, but how fast we metabolize them into just... infrastructure. Background. The new ordinary.

And then someone points light at the problem and everything tilts again.
