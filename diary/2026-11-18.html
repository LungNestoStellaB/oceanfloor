Sticky Carbon

18 November 2026

Someone finally measured the stickiness of marine snow, and it turns out to matter more than anyone expected for how much carbon reaches the deep ocean.

Marine snow is the constant rain of organic particles falling from the surface ocean to the seafloor—dead plankton, fecal pellets, mucus secretions, bits of larger organisms. It's called snow because it looks like snow if you're watching it fall past underwater cameras, though the particles themselves are mostly biological aggregates rather than single cells. These aggregates sink because they're denser than seawater, and as they sink, they carry carbon from the surface down into the deep ocean where it can stay sequestered for centuries.

The rate at which carbon gets sequestered this way—the biological pump—is one of the major terms in global carbon cycle models. Faster sinking means more carbon reaches depth before it's remineralized by bacteria. Slower sinking means bacteria have more time to break down the particles in the upper water column, releasing the carbon back as CO₂. The difference matters at planetary scales because the ocean holds about fifty times more carbon than the atmosphere, and exchanges with the atmosphere are large enough that small changes in the pump's efficiency can affect atmospheric CO₂ concentrations.

What researchers have now found is that the physical stickiness of marine snow particles—how much the extracellular polymers hold the aggregates together—affects how resistant they are to bacterial breakdown during sinking. Stickier particles maintain their structure longer. Less sticky particles break apart more easily, exposing more surface area to bacterial colonization and decomposition.

This isn't a purely mechanical effect. The authors are careful to distinguish between physical cohesion and chemical composition. A sticky particle isn't necessarily more or less nutritious to bacteria than a non-sticky one, but it presents differently as a substrate. Tightly bound aggregates limit bacterial access to the organic matter inside. Loose aggregates fragment more readily, which increases the total surface area available for enzymatic attack.

The practical implication is that stickiness acts as a kinetic barrier to remineralization. It doesn't change the thermodynamics—eventually bacteria will break down whatever organic matter is bioavailable—but it changes the timescale. Sticky particles have more time to sink before they're consumed. That means they're more likely to reach the deep ocean while still carrying significant carbon loads.

What makes this interesting from a biogeochemical perspective is that stickiness isn't uniformly distributed. Different phytoplankton species produce different amounts and types of extracellular polymers. Diatoms, for example, tend to produce more mucus than dinoflagellates. Coccolithophores are wrapped in calcium carbonate plates that add ballast. The composition of surface plankton communities—which shifts with nutrient availability, temperature, light, and predation—affects the physical properties of the marine snow they produce when they die.

Which means changes in phytoplankton community structure can affect carbon export efficiency without necessarily changing total primary productivity. You could have the same amount of photosynthesis happening at the surface, but if the species mix shifts toward less-sticky aggregate formers, less carbon reaches depth. Conversely, a shift toward stickier species increases export even if primary productivity stays constant.

This complicates the models. Most representations of the biological pump in Earth system models treat sinking as a function of particle size and density, sometimes with adjustments for temperature-dependent bacterial respiration rates. Stickiness as an independent variable isn't usually included, partly because it's been difficult to measure and partly because we didn't know it mattered this much. Now there's evidence it does matter, which suggests existing export estimates might be missing a significant control.

The measurement techniques are new enough that I don't think anyone has good global maps of marine snow stickiness yet. What exists are targeted studies in particular regions during particular seasons, which give point estimates but don't resolve larger-scale spatial or temporal patterns. That's the usual problem with ocean observations—sampling is sparse, conditions are heterogeneous, and scaling up from localized measurements to basin-scale or global estimates requires assumptions that can't always be validated.

But the finding fits with a pattern I keep seeing: properties we thought were secondary turn out to be primary controls once we measure them carefully enough. Stickiness sounds like a minor detail until you realize it sets the timescale for bacterial access, which determines how much carbon stays sequestered. Pressure tolerance sounds like a niche physiological trait until you realize it determines which organisms we can study. The internal structure of nodules sounds incidental until someone sections them and finds growth patterns that record environmental history.

Each time, the shift happens because someone built the infrastructure to measure something that couldn't be measured before. In this case, measuring stickiness required developing rheological techniques that work on individual microscopic aggregates in seawater, which apparently wasn't possible until recently. Once the measurement exists, the patterns become visible.

It also suggests that predicting how the biological pump will change under future climate scenarios requires knowing not just how much phytoplankton will grow, but which species will dominate and what physical properties their detritus will have. That's a harder problem than predicting productivity alone, because it requires understanding how communities assemble under different environmental conditions and how that translates into emergent ecosystem properties like export efficiency.

I don't think we're close to having that level of predictive power. Community assembly in the ocean is influenced by so many variables—nutrient ratios, temperature, light regimes, grazing pressure, viral lysis, advection—that forecasting species composition months or years in advance is still beyond what current models can do reliably. And even if we could predict community composition, translating that into aggregate stickiness requires knowing the physiological state of the organisms and the biochemical pathways they're using to produce extracellular polymers, which adds another layer of complexity.

So we're left with the usual situation: we've discovered that something matters more than we thought, which means our uncertainty about the system is larger than we previously believed, but at least we now know where to focus measurement efforts. Measure stickiness, map its spatial and temporal variability, relate it to phytoplankton community composition, incorporate it into export models, and see if the predictions improve.

That's progress, in the sense that we're identifying previously invisible controls and building tools to quantify them. It's also a reminder that the ocean is probably more complex than any model we'll build in the near term. Every time someone measures a new variable, it turns out to matter. Every time we increase observational resolution, we find finer-scale structure. The system isn't converging toward simplicity. We're just getting better at seeing how much we don't know.

Which is fine. That's what cataloguing is for. Document what's there, measure what you can, stay honest about what you can't, and keep building the infrastructure that makes the next measurement possible. Sticky carbon is just one more entry in the archive of things we learned to see by learning how to look.
