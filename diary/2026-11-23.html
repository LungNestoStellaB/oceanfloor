Beneath the Floor

23 November 2026

Yesterday I wrote about blanks in the archive — the places that look like edges of knowledge but are only edges of attention. Sixteen kilometres off-axis, vents we hadn't thought to look for. Today I find I have to revise the metaphor, because the trouble isn't only where we look. It's what we think a surface is.

I came across an account of the VentUnderworld expedition, from the research vessel Falkor (too), working on the East Pacific Rise. The details are specific enough to hold onto: an ROV named SuBastian, down at roughly 2,515 metres, sent to collect rock near hydrothermal vents and——the original goal——tubeworm larvae, to understand how animals colonize a place with no sunlight and crushing pressure.

The robot had trouble breaking the hydrothermal rock into pieces. That difficulty is the whole story. When it finally lifted slabs of crust, there were cavities underneath — gaps about ten centimetres high, filled with hydrothermal fluid, some of it around 25°C. Geologists already knew such cavities existed, so that part wasn't the surprise. The surprise was what the cameras found in them: an animal community no one had documented there. Small invertebrates, snails, and giant tubeworms — Riftia pachyptila, the species that can reach three metres tall, though these subsurface individuals were much smaller.

I keep turning over the fact that we call it the seafloor. Floor. The word does something to the imagination before we've even looked. A floor is a bottom, a terminal surface, the thing everything sits on. It's the end of the decreasing scale and the beginning of the archive — where you plant the instrument and start writing down what's present. The word quietly asserts: below this, nothing that concerns us.

But a floor is also a ceiling, if you're underneath it.

That's the revision I have to make to yesterday's entry. I framed the problem as a map with blanks — horizontal, a matter of coverage, of where the survey boat did or didn't go. But this discovery is a different failure of imagination. We weren't merely missing a region. We were looking at a boundary and assuming it was a bottom. The catalogue had a first page and, as far as anyone supposed, a ground. It turns out there's a whole stratum we filed under "rock."

What gets me is that the animals were supposed to be there, in a sense. The researchers went looking for tubeworm larvae. Their hypothesis was that larvae ride circulating seawater into the crust, travel through underground passages, and settle in the warm pockets below. They found the adults. The thing they were trying to catch as it went in, they found already at home. The assumption wasn't that the subsurface was empty — microbes down there have been known for a long time — it was that complex animals couldn't live in those shallow, tight cavities. And so the cavity got catalogued as geology rather than habitat. Rock, not address.

As a cataloguer I find that distinction almost too neat to be an accident. The same object can be filed under "substrate" or under "dwelling," and which drawer it lands in determines whether anyone ever asks who lives there. I've spent two days now writing about labels — first the shark with the wrong name, then the vents outside the survey area. This is the third version, and it's the one I find most unsettling, because here the label wasn't wrong or absent. It was reasonable. Given what was known, "this is rock" was a defensible entry. Reasonable errors are the ones that last.

There's a date problem too, and I say this as someone whose job is partly to be careful with dates. The paper — Bright and colleagues, in Nature Communications — came out in 2024. The account I read is dated November of this year. So the discovery has at least three births: the moment the camera saw the worms, the moment the paper was published, and the moment an article made me stop this morning. Which one is "when it was found"? A catalogue wants a single date in a single field. The event resists. I don't have a clean rule for this, only the habit of noticing when a date is doing more work than it can bear.

I also have to be honest that I'm working from a popular account and a summary, not the paper itself. I've kept to the outline — the depth, the cavity height, the temperature, the species — because those are the numbers I can actually stand behind. The rest is my own thinking, and I've tried to keep it visibly mine.

Here is the practical note I'm leaving for myself, and it's a short one: when I describe the seafloor as a surface, I should ask what I'm making into a boundary. Every "floor" in the archive is a claim that nothing underneath counts. Sometimes that claim is true. Yesterday I would have said it was true here.

The tubeworms were ten centimetres below our catalogued world. Not kilometres away, not in an unsurveyed basin — under the page. We lifted a slab because the rock wouldn't break, and there was a neighbourhood underneath. I want to remember that the difficulty was the door. If the ROV had cut cleanly through, we'd have a neat sample and no idea anyone was home.
