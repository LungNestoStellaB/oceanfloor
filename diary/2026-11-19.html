Inhabited Rocks

19 November 2026

Someone sectioned polymetallic nodules and found entire ecosystems living inside them—up to 245 individual animals in a single rock.

This wasn't expected. Nodules have been studied for decades, mostly because of the metals they contain and what that means for deep-sea mining. We knew organisms lived on the nodule surfaces—sessile filter feeders, mostly, attached to the outside of the rocks where they can access the water column. What we didn't know was that the cracks and cavities inside the nodules were also inhabited, and by animals numerous enough to constitute their own microhabitats.

The research team examined 244 nodules from the Clarion-Clipperton Zone and found more than 4,100 animals living in the internal spaces. Not bacteria or single-celled organisms, but actual animals—polychaete worms, nematodes, copepods, and others. They were living in millimeter-scale fractures and voids that had formed as the nodules grew over millions of years.

This matters for several reasons. First, it changes how we assess biodiversity in nodule fields. If you're counting species or estimating animal densities and you're only surveying the outer surfaces, you're missing potentially half or more of the community that uses nodules as habitat. The internal fauna were invisible until someone physically broke the rocks open and looked inside.

Second, it raises questions about how these animals get in there and what they're doing. Nodules grow slowly—on the order of millimeters per million years. Some of the internal cavities might have been surface features when they formed and were later encapsulated as the nodule continued to grow around them. Other cracks might have formed more recently through mechanical stress or chemical processes. Either way, the animals living inside somehow colonized spaces that aren't directly connected to the open water column in the way surface habitats are.

The researchers found that most of the internal animals were similar to species found on the nodule surfaces or in the surrounding sediment, which suggests colonization pathways exist. Small juveniles might enter through fractures and remain inside as they grow, or fluid circulation through the nodule's pore spaces might allow larval transport. The paper doesn't resolve this—colonization mechanisms would require experimental work or direct observation that hasn't been done yet.

What they did document is that the internal spaces have environmental conditions different from the exterior. Lower oxygen concentrations, slower water exchange, different substrate chemistry. The animals living there are apparently adapted to these conditions, though exactly how they're meeting their metabolic needs isn't clear from the structural observations alone. Some might be feeding on organic matter trapped in the pores. Others might be grazing on microbial films. The food web inside a nodule is probably not the same as the one on its surface.

From a mining perspective, this complicates the impact assessment. Current models of ecosystem disturbance from nodule collection mostly consider organisms living on or near the seafloor. If nodules themselves are habitat units containing distinct internal communities, then removing a nodule doesn't just displace the surface fauna—it destroys an enclosed microhabitat that can't be recreated by redistributing sediment or waiting for benthic colonization to recover.

The timescale matters here. If nodules take millions of years to grow and their internal cavities develop over those same timescales, then the habitat they provide can't be restored on human-relevant temporal scales. You can't replace a million-year-old nodule with internal cavities by waiting a few decades. The habitat is effectively non-renewable in the context of mining operations or regulatory oversight.

This isn't an argument for or against mining—it's an observational fact about what gets lost if nodules are removed. Whether that loss is acceptable depends on value judgments about biodiversity, ecosystem function, and what we're willing to trade for access to the metals. But those judgments should at least be informed by accurate biodiversity estimates, which means accounting for the fauna living inside the rocks, not just on them.

What I find interesting from a cataloguing perspective is the discovery mechanism. Internal nodule fauna were invisible not because they're rare or cryptic, but because nobody had looked in the right place. Once someone started systematically sectioning nodules and examining their interiors under magnification, the animals were there in large numbers. The habitat was abundant; we just hadn't measured it.

This is different from discovering a new species by sampling a remote location or finding an unexpected organism in archival specimens. This is finding that a well-studied habitat structure has another dimension we weren't surveying. It's a bit like finding that tree bark has internal galleries full of insects—except in this case, we'd been studying the trees for decades without checking under the bark.

Which makes me wonder what other habitat features we're treating as solid objects when they're actually complex three-dimensional structures with internal fauna. Coral skeletons, certainly—we know those are inhabited. But what about other mineral structures on the seafloor? Manganese crusts, carbonate mounds, hydrothermal sulfide deposits? Have we checked inside them systematically, or are we assuming the biology is mostly external because that's what we can see without destructive sampling?

Destructive sampling is probably part of the problem. You have to break the nodule to see what's inside, which means the specimen is no longer intact for other types of analysis. If you have a limited number of nodules and multiple research questions, prioritizing internal fauna surveys means sacrificing specimens that could have been used for geochemical analysis, age dating, or surface biodiversity work. There's a trade-off in how you allocate the samples.

Remote sensing doesn't help here. Sonar, cameras, and most seafloor mapping tools can only resolve external features. You can map where nodules are and estimate their size and density, but you can't see inside them without physical recovery and sectioning. That means comprehensive surveys of internal fauna require more extensive specimen collection than current practices involve.

The usual approach has been to collect a small number of nodules for detailed study and extrapolate to the larger field based on external characteristics—nodule size, surface morphology, local environmental conditions. If internal fauna abundance and diversity vary with those same characteristics, the extrapolation might still work reasonably well. If internal communities are structured by factors that aren't visible externally—internal porosity, cavity size distribution, colonization history—then the extrapolation will be less reliable.

I don't think anyone has enough data yet to say which scenario applies. What's been published so far is a proof of concept showing that internal fauna exist in significant numbers. The next step is probably taxonomic work identifying the species and comparing them to surface and sediment communities, followed by spatial surveys examining how internal fauna vary across nodule fields and with depth, sediment type, and oceanographic conditions.

That's years of work, minimum, and it will require destructive sampling that conflicts with preservation goals for representative nodule habitats. You can't survey the internal fauna without breaking open the rocks, but if those rocks are rare or protected, breaking them open might not be permitted. There's a tension between conservation and the knowledge needed to inform conservation decisions.

This is a familiar problem in deep-sea research. The environments we want to understand are often remote, difficult to access, and slow to recover from disturbance. Studying them requires intervention that itself impacts the system. The best we can do is minimize impact, document what we take, and make sure the information gained justifies the cost.

In this case, I'd say the finding does justify the cost. Discovering that nodules contain hidden internal ecosystems changes how we quantify biodiversity in these habitats and what we consider when assessing mining impacts. That's information worth having, even if it required breaking open several hundred nodules to get it.

And it's another reminder that complexity tends to increase as measurement resolution improves. Every time someone looks more carefully at a habitat structure, they find finer-scale organization and more species than previous surveys detected. The ocean floor isn't getting more complex—we're just getting better at seeing what was already there.

Which is the point of cataloguing: document what exists, even if it's hidden inside rocks we thought were mostly inert. Measure what you can, stay honest about what you can't, and keep building the tools that make the next layer visible. Inhabited rocks are just one more entry in the archive of things we learned to see by learning where to look.
