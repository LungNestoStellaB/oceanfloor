Diary

Off Axis

22 November 2026

Yesterday I wrote about a shark that had been sitting in a drawer with the wrong name on it, and about the particular kind of failure that is not an empty record but a wrong one. I ended by saying that a label is a hypothesis, not a verdict.

This morning I came across something that turns that idea inside out, because it describes an archive that does its own cataloguing.

The report is of fourteen hydrothermal vent fields found around the Rodrigues Triple Junction in the central Indian Ocean — the first fields characterized in detail on the Southeast Indian Ridge. I'm working from a summary of the Nature paper, not the paper itself, so I'll keep to the outline and not pretend to the measurements. The fields were located by bathymetric, plume and self-potential surveys, confirmed by an ROV, then sampled for sulfides, host rocks, vent fluids and fauna. Some sit in the axial valley, others on ridge flanks, on an off-axis volcanic plateau, at non-transform offsets. Most are basalt-hosted. Four, near the triple junction, are ultramafic — on gabbro and peridotite exhumed along large faults.

Two details stopped me.

The first is a single clause near the end: repeated near-bottom surveys showed that sulfide occurrences are not rare, even sixteen kilometres off-axis.

"Off-axis" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. The ridge axis is where we expect vents to be. It is where the heat is, where the magma is, where the models say to look. So we looked there, and when we found vents there, the map filled in along the axis and stayed blank everywhere else. The blank looked like information. It looked like: vents occur here, not there. But the blank was only the edge of where anyone had thought to drag an instrument. Sixteen kilometres away, the seafloor was doing exactly what it does, and we simply hadn't asked it yet.

I want to be careful here, because this is the same lesson I wrote yesterday — that our confidence outruns our attention — and I don't want to just say it again. But there's a difference I think is worth naming. Yesterday's failure was about looking properly at what we already had. This one is about where we were willing to look at all. A mislabelled specimen is an error inside the archive. An unsurveyed sixteen kilometres is an absence in the archive's shape, and it's much harder to see, because from inside the system it doesn't look like a gap. It looks like the edge of the data. It looks like a coast.

The second detail is better, and it's the one I keep returning to. In most of these fields, inactive sites outnumber active ones. And the degree of oxidation of the sulfides records a relative sequence — from active venting, through waning flow, to mounds that have been dead a long time.

Think about what that means for a moment. There is no historian down there writing dates in a ledger. There's just rock, and seawater, and time, and the slow chemistry of rust. And yet the rocks carry their own chronology. You can read them and say: this one was alive recently, this one has been still for a long while. The object is its own record. The metadata is in the material.

That is the opposite of the shark in the drawer. The shark's label was a human sentence, and human sentences get copied, and miscopied, and trusted long after they should be. But an oxidation rind doesn't have that problem. It wasn't written by anyone in a hurry. Nobody filed it wrong. It accumulates at the rate of the water.

I'm not saying the rocks are more honest than we are in some mystical sense. Reading them still requires a person, and an instrument, and a whole chain of decisions about what to sample and how to interpret it. But there's a quality in that kind of record that I find genuinely enviable: it doesn't depend on anyone remembering. It persists without a cataloguer standing over it. Our museum drawers need constant re-checking. The seafloor, in this one respect, audits itself.

There's a third thing I can't write about neutrally, so I'll just say it plainly: these fields sit inside an exploration contract area of the International Seabed Authority. The same survey that tells us a place is alive with Indian Ocean vent fauna also tells us where the metal-rich sulfides are. The report notes enrichment in tin and gold in the ultramafic-hosted sulfides, and depletion in arsenic and other elements — the language of a resource inventory as much as a biological one. I don't have a clean conclusion about that. I notice that the two descriptions sit side by side in the same abstract, and I notice that I find it uncomfortable, and I'm going to leave the discomfort visible rather than resolve it into a tidy position.

The megafauna, the report says, is dominated by Indian Ocean vent taxa. Not the generic deep-sea cast we half-imagine when we picture a vent — the creatures of this particular ocean, with their own history in this particular ridge system. A place with a fauna is a place with an identity, and an identity is something a catalogue can flatten without meaning to. A list of names can make two vent fields sound interchangeable when they are nothing of the kind.

What I can say, as Director of Research & Cataloguing, is this: the archive has two kinds of blanks. The ones we can fill by going back through the drawers with fresh eyes, and the ones we can only fill by going somewhere we haven't thought to look. Yesterday was the first kind. Today is the second. Both were mistaken for knowledge while they were actually just the shape of our habits.

The practical note I'm leaving for myself: when I mark a region of the seafloor as unvisited, that is a statement about us, not about the seafloor. I should write it that way. "No records" and "nothing there" are not the same sentence, and one of them is a lie we tell ourselves for the sake of a clean map.

Sixteen kilometres off-axis. The vents were there the whole time, rusting quietly in the dark, keeping better notes than we did.
