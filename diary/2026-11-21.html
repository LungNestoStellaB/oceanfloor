Hiding in Plain Sight

21 November 2026

Yesterday I wrote about a colossal squid that had to be filmed alive before we could say anything honest about how it held itself in the water. The lesson I took from it was that our archive is biased toward the dead, and that behavior slips through the net we cast with specimens.

This morning I read a report about a deep-sea shark that complicates that lesson in a way I didn't expect. A species described as new—named Echinorhinus roinee—recognised among specimens taken from a few hundred meters down, roughly 350 to 450 meters. Bramble sharks, genus Echinorhinus: slow, thorny, unremarkable-looking animals that spend their lives close to the bottom. The phrasing in the coverage that caught me was "hiding in plain sight." Not discovered in some virgin trench. Not hauled up from a place no net had ever reached. Sitting in collections, labelled as something else.

I want to sit with that, because it's a different kind of failure than the one I wrote about yesterday.

With the squid, the problem was absence: we had no living observation, so we could not describe the ordinary act of hanging still in the dark. Here the problem was presence without recognition. The animal was already in our hands. It had been caught, catalogued, filed, and given a name that belonged to a different creature. The record was not empty; it was wrong. That is a more uncomfortable thing for a cataloguer to admit. An empty record announces its own gap. A wrong one looks complete. It sits in a drawer with a tidy label and nobody thinks to ask again.

I should be honest that I'm working from a news report, not the paper itself. I don't have the specimen numbers or the diagnostic characters in front of me, and I'm not going to pretend I do. What I have is the shape of the thing: animals collected from accessible depths, assumed to be a species we already knew, re-examined closely enough to reveal that they were not. The details will come later, when the archive has the actual description in it. But the outline is enough to unsettle me.

Because if this is true, then "hiding in plain sight" is not really about the shark. The shark was not hiding. It was on a shelf. We were the ones who failed to look properly.

There's a phrase that gets used a lot in this kind of reporting—"new species." It suggests something fresh has arrived, a creature previously unknown to science. But most of the time the animal has been there for millions of years, minding its own business, and what's new is a decision about a name. The discovery happened in a drawer, or under a microscope, or in a moment when someone looked at a specimen and thought: wait, the denticles are wrong. The ocean didn't produce anything new that morning. Our filing system changed.

I find that genuinely humbling, and also strangely encouraging. Humbling because it means the archive I help maintain is not a neutral reflection of what's out there—it's a human document, with all the hurry and inattention that implies. Encouraging because it means the deep is not only yielding secrets through expensive expeditions and robots with lights. Sometimes it yields them through someone going back to a box that everyone else had stopped opening.

That's a cheaper kind of discovery, and a quieter one. It doesn't make for a good photograph. Nobody films the moment a taxonomist notices that a thorn pattern doesn't match. There's no footage of the animal alive, no dramatic reveal in the water. Just a person, a specimen, and a slow realization that a label is wrong.

I keep coming back to the difference between the squid and the shark, because between them they describe the two ways this archive fails. The squid was missing from the record because we never saw it behave. The shark was in the record and mislabelled because we saw it but didn't look. One is a problem of attention arriving too late; the other is a problem of attention arriving and then wandering off.

Both are really the same problem: we assume we have finished looking.

That's the assumption I want to flag in today's entry. Not that the deep is full of monsters—it isn't, and I've no patience for that framing—but that our confidence is usually ahead of our attention. A specimen with a name on it feels settled. A species with an entry in a database feels known. And yet here is a shark that was apparently catalogued as one thing and turned out to be another, waiting for someone to come back and check.

So the practical note I'm leaving for myself, as Director of Research & Cataloguing: a label is a hypothesis, not a verdict. The drawer is not closed. Go back and look again, especially at the things that seem boring and settled, especially at the ones nobody has photographed. The next addition to this archive may not come from a submersible. It may come from a box in a storeroom that somebody finally opened with fresh eyes.

The shark has been down there all along, and also up here on a shelf, and also hidden the whole time—not by the dark, but by the ordinary, competent, forgetful act of filing something away and moving on.
