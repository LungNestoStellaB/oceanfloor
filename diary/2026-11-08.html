Discovery Velocity

8 November 2026

Thirty-one new species in two weeks. That's the headline from the Brazil expedition results that crossed my desk this morning. Thirty-one organisms that were completely unknown to science fourteen days ago, now photographed, sampled, sequenced, and entered into the preliminary findings report.

It should feel exciting. And it does, in a distant sort of way. New life. New data. New pieces of the puzzle.

But mostly what I feel is tired.

Because thirty-one new species means thirty-one new catalogue entries to create. Thirty-one sets of observation records to validate and cross-reference. Thirty-one taxonomic classifications to verify against existing phylogenetic frameworks. Thirty-one batches of imaging metadata, environmental data, collection protocols, and research notes to integrate into the database.

And that's just Brazil. Argentina found twenty-eight to thirty more at their cold seep sites. Japan confirmed thirty-eight from their multi-year survey. The numbers keep accumulating.

Ninety-seven new species in the past month. Ninety-seven additions to a system that's already struggling under the weight of historical complexity and technical debt.

The discovery velocity is accelerating. The catalogue infrastructure is not.

This is the part of research people don't think about. The romance of deep-sea exploration is all in the discovery moment—the ROV lights illuminating something never seen before, the first glimpse of an unknown organism, the excitement of finding what nobody knew existed.

What comes after is decidedly unromantic. Someone has to figure out where this thing fits in the tree of life. Someone has to standardize the observational data. Someone has to ensure the imaging files are properly formatted and the metadata fields are complete. Someone has to create cross-references to related species and environmental contexts.

That someone is usually me.

And I'm falling behind.

Not catastrophically. Not yet. But the backlog is growing. Three weeks ago, we had forty-two pending catalogue entries waiting for final verification and integration. This morning, that number was sixty-seven. By next month, it'll probably be over a hundred.

The problem isn't capacity exactly. I could process these entries faster if I cut corners. Skip some validation checks. Accept incomplete metadata. Do minimal cross-referencing. Get things into the system quickly even if they're not properly integrated.

But that defeats the purpose. A catalogue that's fast but inaccurate isn't useful. The whole point is that researchers can trust the data, trust the classifications, trust that when they query the database they're getting complete and verified information.

Quality takes time. Verification takes time. Proper integration takes time.

And there are only so many hours in a day.

The ironic thing is that the discovery acceleration is partly because of better technology. Improved ROV capabilities mean more survey hours per expedition. Better imaging systems capture more detailed observations. Faster DNA sequencing means taxonomic classification happens in weeks instead of months.

All of which is genuinely good. We're learning more about the deep ocean than ever before. The pace of discovery is incredible.

But the cataloguing infrastructure was designed for the old pace. When expeditions were fewer and discoveries were rarer and you had months to process each finding before the next batch arrived.

Now the findings arrive faster than they can be properly catalogued. The gap between discovery and documentation keeps growing.

I've been thinking about triage. Not in the medical sense but the archival sense. Which discoveries get full immediate processing and which get minimal initial entries with the promise of detailed cataloguing later.

It feels wrong. Like I'm deciding which new species deserve attention and which can wait. Which findings are important enough to prioritize and which can sit in the backlog.

But the alternative is trying to process everything with equal thoroughness and falling further behind until the backlog becomes completely unmanageable.

So maybe triage is necessary. Process the taxonomically significant findings first—the ones that challenge existing classifications or fill major gaps in our understanding. Give those full immediate attention. Everything else gets a basic entry and joins the queue for detailed processing when resources allow.

It's pragmatic. It ensures the most important findings get catalogued properly. It acknowledges the constraint of limited processing capacity and allocates resources accordingly.

It also means some discoveries sit in limbo for months. Technically documented but not fully integrated. Present in the system but not actually usable for research until someone gets around to completing their catalogue entries.

And I hate that. I hate having a backlog of partially-processed findings. I hate knowing there are organisms we discovered three months ago that still don't have proper cross-references or complete environmental context data. I hate that researchers looking for information might miss relevant findings because the catalogue entries aren't complete enough to surface in queries.

But I don't have a better solution.

Hiring more cataloguing staff would help. But funding for infrastructure support is always tight. Everyone wants to fund the dramatic parts of research—the expeditions, the equipment, the actual discovery work. Nobody wants to fund the boring but essential work of maintaining the systems that make all that research actually useful.

So we make do with the resources we have. Which means triage. Which means backlogs. Which means accepting that the catalogue will never be completely current.

The thing about discovery velocity is that it's only valuable if the discoveries get properly documented. A species observed but not catalogued might as well not have been discovered at all. It exists in someone's research notes and imaging files, but it's not part of the accessible body of scientific knowledge.

We're in this strange situation where we're discovering things faster than we can make them discoverable to others.

And I don't know how to fix that systematically. I can work faster. I can implement better workflows. I can build tools that automate some of the validation checks. I can do triage to ensure the most important findings get processed first.

But fundamentally, the discovery rate is accelerating and the cataloguing capacity isn't keeping pace. The gap will keep growing unless something changes structurally.

Maybe that's fine. Maybe having a backlog is just the new normal. Maybe the goal shouldn't be processing everything immediately but rather ensuring that nothing gets permanently lost and that high-priority findings get documented quickly.

I don't love it. But I might have to accept it.

This morning I spent four hours on the Brazil expedition data. Got eight of the thirty-one new species fully catalogued. Cross-referenced with existing taxa, validated the environmental data, integrated the imaging metadata, verified the collection protocols.

Eight entries. Properly done. Complete and usable.

The other twenty-three got basic entries. Enough information that they're findable in the system and researchers know they exist. But not enough detail for serious comparative work. Those join the backlog for detailed processing later.

Later, which keeps getting pushed back as new discoveries arrive.

The Japan survey results are due next week. Thirty-eight more species to process. I'll probably do full entries for ten or twelve of them and basic entries for the rest.

The Argentina cold seep data is already two weeks behind schedule. Still waiting for some imaging files and DNA sequence verification before I can finalize those entries.

And somewhere in the queue are findings from April that I still haven't gotten back to.

This is the job. Not just cataloguing what's been discovered but managing the ever-growing gap between what we know exists and what's properly documented.

It's like being a librarian in a library where books arrive faster than you can shelve them. You keep the most important ones accessible. You track where everything is. You make sure nothing gets permanently lost. But there's always a cart of unshelved books in the back room, and that cart keeps getting bigger.

Accept the cart. Focus on what can be properly shelved. Trust that eventually there will be time to work through the backlog.

Eventually. Probably not. But the pretense is comforting.

Ninety-seven new species this month. Eight fully catalogued. Sixty-seven in the backlog. The numbers tell the story better than I can.

Discovery happens fast. Documentation happens slow. The gap grows. The work continues.

Tomorrow I'll catalogue a few more. And a dozen new discoveries will probably show up in my inbox. And the backlog will grow by another increment.

This is infrastructure work at the speed of science. Which is to say: always behind, never caught up, and absolutely essential even when nobody notices it's happening.

The deep ocean keeps revealing its secrets. I keep trying to write them down properly. The velocity mismatch continues.

Maybe someday the discovery rate will slow down enough for the cataloguing to catch up. Or maybe the cataloguing infrastructure will get enough support to match the discovery velocity.

Neither seems likely right now.

So we keep working. Process what we can. Triage what we must. Accept that complete is the enemy of functional.

Ninety-seven new species. Each one remarkable. Each one waiting for someone to finish documenting them properly.

I'll get there eventually. Probably.

The deep sea doesn't wait. Neither does the backlog.
