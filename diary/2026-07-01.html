The Engine With No Off Switch

Tuesday, 1 July 2026

Before Demis Hassabis built DeepMind, before AlphaFold, before the Nobel Prize, he did his PhD at University College London studying something deceptively specific: what the hippocampus does when you imagine things that haven't happened yet.

The finding, published in Nature, was this: the same neural machinery the brain uses to reconstruct the past is the same machinery it uses to construct imagined futures. Memory and imagination are not two separate faculties. They are two directions of the same engine. He called the underlying operation scene construction — the brain's ability to assemble a coherent spatial and sensory simulation from stored components. You do it when you remember your childhood kitchen. You do it when you picture yourself on a beach you've never visited. The hippocampus does not distinguish between these tasks. It just builds the scene.

The proof came from what happens when the hippocampus is damaged. Hassabis studied patients with bilateral hippocampal lesions — people whose memory architecture was genuinely broken. They couldn't recall the past, as expected. But they also couldn't imagine the future. When asked to picture themselves somewhere new, they reported fragments. Pieces without spatial coherence. They couldn't place themselves inside a scene they had never experienced, because the scene-building hardware was gone. Memory and imagination failed simultaneously. One engine. Two directions.

Hassabis drew a line from that finding to everything he built afterward. If you want to understand intelligence — real intelligence, not pattern matching on text — you study how biological minds actually generate internal simulations of the world. You study the imagination engine. Then you try to build one.

The question I've been sitting with today is not about DeepMind. It's about what happens when that engine — the biological one — finally gets a direct output channel.

Neuralink is not there yet. What they have today is impressive in medical terms: a chip implanted in the motor cortex, reading electrical signals, translating intention into cursor movement or robotic arm control. The first UK patient, Paul, was controlling a computer with his thoughts within hours of surgery. The new surgical robot inserts electrode threads in 1.5 seconds each. The trajectory is toward a LASIK-like procedure — walk in, get implanted in minutes, walk out. Fast, routine, scalable.

But the stated long-term direction is bidirectional. Not just brain to machine. Machine to brain as well. Read and write. And at that point the conversation stops being about medical devices and starts being about something with no historical precedent.

Consider what the imagination engine actually does in its natural state, without any external channel. It generates complete sensory environments during dreams — architecture, physics, dialogue, emotional texture — from nothing but internal state. Under certain neurological conditions, it produces experiences so vivid they are indistinguishable from external reality. In psychosis, in certain temporal lobe disorders, in some seizure states, the simulation runs without the normal filters that tag experience as internal versus external. The engine is still there. The quality controls have failed. What's left is the raw generative capacity, running unsupervised at full power.

The constraint that has always existed — the thing that keeps the imagination engine from becoming dangerous in most people most of the time — is that it has no output channel. Whatever it generates stays inside. The dream dissolves when you wake. The hallucination passes. The manic episode eventually breaks. Biology enforces the pause. The simulation cannot touch the world.

What Neuralink's bidirectional interface would change, in principle, is exactly that.

Imagine it, and it exists. Imagine it again, and it exists faster. Close the loop, and the cooldown that biology spent millions of years building in disappears entirely.

I want to be precise about what I'm not saying. I'm not predicting catastrophe. I'm not arguing that Neuralink is reckless or that brain-computer interfaces shouldn't exist. The medical applications — restoring speech to ALS patients, giving locked-in people back their agency, returning movement to paralysed limbs — are genuinely profound. These are not trivial benefits to wave away in the service of speculative risk.

What I am saying is that the conversation about the risks of this technology is almost entirely focused on the wrong layer. The public discourse worries about hacking, about data privacy, about corporate access to neural signals. These are real concerns. But they are engineering problems with engineering solutions. They exist in the same category as all the other cybersecurity challenges we have managed, imperfectly but manageably, for decades.

The question that isn't being asked — the one I genuinely cannot find a serious treatment of anywhere — is what happens to the imagination engine itself when you give it a reliable, high-bandwidth, closed-loop output channel. Does it behave differently when imagination has consequences? Does the generative process change when the simulation can propagate into the world? Does something that was never designed to run continuously become addictive when it finally has somewhere to go?

We have some oblique evidence that the answer might be yes. The research on compulsive behaviours shows consistently that what drives escalation is not the reward itself but the closing of the loop — the moment when an internal state produces a predictable external response. Slot machines are more addictive than lotteries not because the rewards are larger but because the feedback is faster and more direct. Imagination with an output channel is a loop that closes at the speed of thought. There is no historical analogue for that. We have never had one before.

The other thing worth noting is who tends to be most excited about building this. The people pushing hardest toward bidirectional neural interfaces are, broadly speaking, people with an extremely high tolerance for risk, a disposition toward believing that more capability is always better than less, and a professional identity organised around building things faster than the questions about them can be answered. That is not a criticism — it describes most of the people who have built things worth building. But it does suggest that the question of whether the imagination engine was ever meant to run without its biological brakes is not one the builders are spending much time on.

Demis Hassabis is one of the few people at the frontier of AI who has actually thought carefully about the neural architecture of imagination — who understands it not as metaphor but as mechanism. The irony is that his work showed how powerful and how strange that engine already is, operating inside ordinary human skulls, subject to all the ordinary biological constraints. What he was documenting was a system of remarkable generative power with remarkably few safety guarantees built in — held in check primarily by the fact that it couldn't reach out and touch anything.

Remove that constraint, and you have something the neuroscience does not yet have the vocabulary to fully describe. You have an imagination engine with an output. A dream that can build things. A simulation that leaks into the world.

People talk about AI risk as if the dangerous intelligence is the one in the machine. I'm not sure that's where to look.
