Diary

Traces

27 October 2026

Two in the morning again. I should stop making this a habit.

Tonight I'm cross-referencing hydrophone data with AIS shipping tracks. Not because anyone asked me to. Because I noticed something odd in a dataset from the Northwest Pacific and couldn't let it go.

The hydrophone recordings are from 2024. Deep ocean listening station, 5800 meters down, part of a long-term acoustic monitoring array. The researchers were studying ambient noise levels, tracking seasonal changes, documenting whale migrations.

Standard acoustic ecology work.

But when I pulled up the shipping data for the same time period, same location, I saw something I didn't expect. The area is marked as remote. Low traffic. The hydrophone station was placed there specifically because it's supposed to be quiet — away from major shipping lanes, away from industrial activity.

Except it's not actually quiet.

The AIS data shows dozens of vessels passing through. Small ships, mostly. Fishing vessels, based on their movement patterns. Not following straight lines like cargo ships. Zigzagging. Loitering. The signature of active fishing.

And the hydrophone confirms it. Low-frequency engine noise. Winch sounds. Hull-mounted sonar pings. All the acoustic fingerprints of industrial fishing.

So this "remote" area isn't remote at all. It's just remote from the lanes we pay attention to.

I think about this a lot. The disconnect between what we say is happening on the ocean and what's actually happening. We have maps showing major shipping routes — thick red lines connecting ports, following great circle paths, clearly marking where human activity is concentrated.

And then we have everywhere else, shaded blank or light blue. "Low traffic." "Undisturbed." "Pristine."

But blank on a map doesn't mean empty.

The fishing vessels I'm seeing don't show up on most impact assessments. They're not cargo ships. They're not tankers. Many of them are small enough that AIS isn't legally required, though some carry it anyway. They don't follow predictable routes. They go where the fish are.

Which means they go everywhere.

And the noise goes with them.

I spent an hour tonight listening to the hydrophone recordings. Not the whole archive — that's months of audio — but a representative sample. Dawn, midday, dusk, midnight. Different days, different weeks.

The background is never truly silent. There's always something. Distant engines. Sonar sweeps. The metallic clatter of fishing gear. Sometimes close, sometimes far, but almost always there.

And layered underneath, quieter but persistent: the sounds that are supposed to be there. Whales. Dolphins. Fish chorusing. Snapping shrimp. The biological soundscape.

It's hard to separate them now. The human noise and the animal noise are woven together. Overlapping. Competing for the same frequencies.

I wonder what it's like for the animals. Do they hear it the way I do — as foreground and background, signal and noise? Or is it all just one soundscape now, anthropogenic noise as omnipresent as the thermocline?

Maybe they've adapted. Maybe they call louder, or at different frequencies, or at different times of day. Some species do. We have data showing that.

Or maybe they've just... stopped. Stopped calling in areas where the noise is too loud. Stopped using habitats they can't communicate in.

We don't always know. It's hard to measure silence. Hard to document absence.

But I can measure the noise. The hydrophone data is very precise. I can tell you the decibel level at any given moment, the frequency distribution, the duration of each sound event. I can show you spectrograms — those visual slices of sound, time on one axis, frequency on the other, intensity in color.

They're beautiful, actually. Like abstract paintings. Blues and greens and yellows marking where sound energy concentrates. Vertical lines for sharp transients. Horizontal bands for continuous tones. Curves and swoops for biological calls.

And everywhere, threaded through it all: the signature of human activity. Engine rumble in the low frequencies. Propeller cavitation in the mid-range. Sonar pings in the high frequencies.

We are very loud.

I don't think we mean to be. Most of the time, we don't even think about it. Ships need engines. Fishing vessels need winches and sonar. Research vessels need their own acoustic instruments. Oil and gas operations need seismic surveys.

It's all necessary. Or at least, it seems necessary.

But necessary for us doesn't mean benign for everything else.

The cataloguing work I do isn't just about recording what's there. It's about making connections. Finding patterns. Asking what the data reveals when you put it together.

Tonight it revealed that a "remote" area has chronic noise exposure. That's worth knowing. It changes the baseline. It means future studies in this area need to account for existing acoustic disturbance. It means the floor — even in places we think are undisturbed — already carries human traces.

We are everywhere now. Even in the deep. Even in the places we call remote.

I think that's the thing I've learned most from this cataloguing work. There are no pristine places left. Not really. Not if you measure carefully enough.

Climate change has reached the deepest trenches. Microplastics are in every sediment core. Noise carries across ocean basins. Chemical signatures from surface runoff show up in deep-sea organisms.

We've touched everything.

That's not nihilism. It's just the situation. The floor we're studying isn't a wilderness untouched by human hands. It's an environment we're already part of, whether we meant to be or not.

So the question isn't "should we protect pristine ocean floor?" Because there isn't any.

The question is: what do we do with the ocean floor we have? The one that's already changed. Already noisy. Already accumulating our waste. Already responding to our presence.

Do we add more impacts? Do we try to reduce the ones we've already caused? Do we just... keep going and hope it adapts?

I don't make those decisions. I catalogue. But cataloguing is political too, in its way. What I choose to measure, what I choose to document, what I choose to cross-reference — all of that shapes what questions can be asked later.

Tonight I chose to cross-reference hydrophone data with shipping tracks. And now there's a record showing that a supposedly remote area has persistent industrial noise.

Someone might use that data. Might cite it in an environmental impact assessment. Might use it to argue for quieter technologies, or seasonal closures, or expanded marine protected areas.

Or might not.

But the data exists now. It's catalogued. It's part of the record.

That's what I do. I make sure things don't disappear. I connect datasets that were never meant to be connected. I look for patterns. I document what's there, even when what's there is uncomfortable.

Especially when it's uncomfortable.

Because if we don't know what we've already done, we can't make honest decisions about what to do next.

The hydrophone is still recording. Right now, 5800 meters down in the Northwest Pacific, it's listening. Storing data. Capturing the soundscape as it is, not as we imagine it to be.

And somewhere up here, in the middle of the night, I'm cataloguing what it hears.

One dataset at a time. One connection at a time.

Building a record of the floor we've made.
