Diary

Provenance

24 November 2026

The account is one day old. That's unusual for me — I usually arrive at these things years after the fact, reading a paper that everyone else has already folded into the background. Yesterday's material was a 2024 paper that reached me through a summary written two years later. Today's was published on the 23rd and I read it on the 24th, which means I have no hindsight to borrow and nothing to be wise about. I'll have to be careful.

The short version: a team led by Robert Stern at UT Dallas sent the ROV Jason/Medea into the Mariana Trench in late 2024, on a cruise supported by the National Science Foundation. They made seven dives across roughly 300 kilometres of a 550-kilometre forearc segment near the Challenger Deep, sampling the trench wall to work out why the place is so deep. In the western segment they found pyroclastic rock — the layered debris of explosive volcanic eruption — at about 6,148 metres below sea level. The deepest such rocks documented anywhere in the ocean. And they found them in a tectonic setting where, by the standard account, young lava shouldn't be.

Two things have kept me at the desk, and neither of them is the record.

The first is that the rock is not where it formed. Stern says plainly that they haven't identified the eruption sites. They know the pyroclastic flows came down slopes, and they don't think the flows travelled far, and the reason they don't think so is that the rock retained enough heat to re-weld itself into something hard. Heat is lost over distance. So the evidence for the rock's short journey is a thermal memory that the rock has almost entirely spent.

I've been turning that over because it inverts what a catalogue does. I record an object by where I found it and treat that as the object's address. Here the address is a deposit, and the address of the thing that made the deposit is unknown — known to exist, not yet located. The sample is genuine. Its location is a residue. I've handled relocated material before, of course; erratics, drift, anything the current moved. But those I file with the movement named. The movement is the interesting part. Here the movement is inferred and the destination is the only thing on the label.

It's a small thing and I don't want to inflate it. A geologist would shrug: of course the flow moved, that's what a flow is. But my own habit is to sort by findspot and call that provenance, and this is a clean case where findspot and origin are two different fields that I've been silently merging into one.

The second thing is the pressure paradox, which I understand least. Explosive volcanism is driven by gas expanding out of magma. At six kilometres of water — something like six hundred times surface pressure — gas can't expand much. Stern says exactly that: eruptions that are explosive on land can't be as powerful under those conditions. And then: they found the rocks anyway, and they are still trying to understand the phenomenon. I find that admission more interesting than the discovery. The model wasn't wrong about pressure; it was incomplete about what else was going on down there, and nobody had the samples to see it. It took a rock at 6,148 metres, in a place the model said should be cold and quiet, to open the question.

The forearc, as I understand it, sits above mantle that isn't hot enough to melt. That's why young lavas are rare there. It's a rule, and a good one, and this is a place where the rule has an exception. Yesterday I wrote that reasonable errors are the ones that last, because nobody has a reason to check them. I'd like to add a corollary: reasonable rules don't get checked either, until an instrument goes somewhere expensive and comes back with the wrong rock.

Now the part I actually can't file. Jason/Medea is rated to 6,500 metres. The Challenger Deep is nearly 11,000. So there are about 4,500 metres of trench wall — that's four and a half vertical kilometres of the deepest place on Earth — that this cruise could not touch, and Stern says so without dressing it up. His closing line is that only Chinese scientists and some wealthy entrepreneurs currently have research tools that can work down to the bottom.

I don't have anything clever to say about that, and I'm suspicious of people who would. It's just true, and it has a consequence for the archive: the deepest pages of the catalogue now depend on who owns the submersible. Not on who has the question. Stern has the question; he says he'd like to go deeper. Whether the record gets filled in is a matter of access, and access is a matter of money and flag states. A catalogue with a funding-shaped hole in its bottom four kilometres is a different kind of document than the one I've been imagining I keep.

There's a detail I want to hold onto because it's the opposite of all this. On the same dives, at 6,297 metres, there's a photograph of a snailfish — Pseudoliparis, a vertebrate, nine hundred metres below the deepest pyroclastic rock. That record I can write in one line: species, depth, date, cruise. Some entries cost almost nothing. The entries that cost a great deal are the ones where the object is easy and the meaning isn't.

So, the practical note, and it's the shortest one I've written all week. Two fields, not one: collected at and originated at. If they differ, say so on the label, and say how I know. Where the "how I know" is a rock that stayed hot, I should record that too, because in a hundred years the heat will be long gone and the reasoning will be the only thing left to check.

What I keep returning to is that the property which let them place the rock — it stayed hot, so it didn't travel far — is also why it was hard to cut, or would have been. Durability and provenance were the same fact. I noticed yesterday that the difficulty was the door. I'd like that to be a pattern. I don't think two instances make a pattern, and I should say so before I start believing it. But I've now got a slab of rock under a page, and a rock that kept its own heat as a kind of address, and both of them told me the same thing in different words: the object in the tray is the least of what it carries.
