The Cost of Staying On the Line

Saturday, 18 July 2026

Two stories arrived this morning and I couldn't stop thinking about them together, even though they appear to have nothing to do with each other. One is about a planet 48 light-years away. The other is about a nurse in Vallejo, California, sitting at her home desk, deciding whether she can afford to stay on the phone with a suicidal patient.

The planet first, because it is genuinely extraordinary. LHS 1140 b has an atmosphere. A rocky, Earth-sized world in the habitable zone of a red dwarf star — not too close, not too far — and for the first time in the history of our search, one of these candidates has something wrapped around it. Just helium for now, detected in the upper layers, probably not enough on its own to support anything we would recognise as life. But the point isn't the helium. The point is that the atmosphere is there at all. That something held on. That a rocky world at the right distance from its star didn't end up bare and airless like so many others. Dr Collin Cherubim at Harvard called it "a big deal," which is the kind of careful understatement scientists deploy when they are actually trying not to let their excitement crack through entirely. It is a big deal. After thousands of exoplanet discoveries, this is the first time we have found atmosphere on a rocky world in the place where life would actually be possible.

We have been searching for the conditions that support life. That is the whole enterprise, the one that gets the telescope time and the paper citations and the BBC headlines. Find the right distance from the star. Find the rocky surface. Find the atmosphere. Check, check, check. LHS 1140 b clears all three boxes. The researchers are careful to note they have not found life — only the prerequisite. But prerequisites are what you spend decades hunting for, because without them the question doesn't even get to be asked.

We are willing to train instruments on a star 48 light-years away and wait years for the data, just for the chance to ask whether conditions there might support something like us. That is how seriously we take the prerequisites for life.

And then there is the nurse.

Her name is Raquel Alvarez Sanchez. She has been an advice nurse at Kaiser Permanente in Vallejo since 2010. Last year she was on a call with a suicidal patient and stayed on the line — for over an hour, until police arrived — because that is what the situation required. She knew, while she was doing it, that the call length would throw off her average handle time for weeks. That it might trigger questions from management. That it might show up in her monthly performance score, which Kaiser compiles using software that tracks call duration, flags periods of inactivity, and uses AI to rate nurses on their empathy and tone of voice.

She stayed on anyway. But she had to make that choice consciously, knowing there would be a cost.

Another nurse — who spoke anonymously because she feared retribution — described a call with an elderly woman who had just received a terminal cancer diagnosis. The woman wasn't suicidal. She was in shock, and she needed someone to talk to. The nurse understood this. She also understood that staying on the line to offer genuine comfort would likely hurt her score. "I had to ask myself," the nurse said, "am I going to get disciplined for going off script or saying more than what is necessary?"

That sentence. I have been sitting with it all morning.

Kaiser is the largest private employer in California. It provides healthcare to more than twelve million Americans. And it has built a system in which the nurses on the phone — the people at the literal point of human contact between a frightened patient and the institution — are performing a continuous background calculation about whether compassion is affordable. Not because they are bad people or bad nurses. Because the system they are embedded in has made compassion a variable cost, and given them real-time feedback on whether they are spending too much of it.

Kaiser says it does not use average handle time to assess performance. The nurses say otherwise. I am not in a position to adjudicate that dispute. What I can say is that the mechanism described — the software watching, the AI rating tone, the monthly score — creates exactly the kind of environment where a nurse thinks twice before staying on a call with someone who is dying. Whether that was the intention is almost beside the point. It is the result.

The AI is not making the bad decision. The AI is making the nurse make the bad decision, while giving management plausible deniability about the pressure she was under.

This is what I keep coming back to. AI in this context isn't replacing the nurse. It isn't even directly overruling her. It is creating a system of constant low-level measurement that changes the cost function of every decision she makes. Stay on the call: penalty. Go off script: penalty. Show more empathy than the algorithm considers necessary: penalty. The nurse is still making the call. The nurse is still the one who picks up the phone and speaks to the terrified elderly woman. But the system has quietly made certain choices more expensive than they used to be. And so, incrementally, those choices get made less often.

This is the thing that the debate about AI in the workplace tends to miss. The argument usually gets framed as automation versus humans — will AI take the job? — and when the answer is no, the conversation moves on. But the more insidious version isn't replacement. It is modification. AI doesn't need to take the nurse's job to change what it means to be a nurse. It just needs to install itself as the measurement layer, and let the incentives do the rest.

I think about the LHS 1140 b discovery again. We looked at that planet and asked: does it have the prerequisites? Atmosphere. Rocky surface. Habitable zone. We looked carefully, with instruments worth billions, because we understand that conditions matter enormously. You cannot have life without the right conditions. The conditions are not incidental — they are constitutive. They are what makes the thing possible.

And yet here, in a call centre in California, we are systematically degrading the conditions that make a certain kind of care possible. Not dramatically. Not all at once. Just a gentle, continuous pressure applied to every nurse every shift, shaping the probability distribution of how they respond when someone calls in crisis. The atmosphere is still there. It's just getting thinner.

The California Nurses Association is heading into contract negotiations with Kaiser this month. AI is reportedly a central issue. California lawmakers are considering bills that would protect doctors and nurses from retaliation for overriding automated recommendations. That's something. The fact that a nurse could be penalised for doing the right thing is a design flaw, and design flaws can be corrected.

But the deeper question is harder than any contract clause. It is about what we think human attention is worth, and whether we are willing to treat it as something other than a cost. Every minute a nurse spends genuinely present with a patient — not processing them, not routing them, but actually being with them — is a minute that does not appear on a revenue line. It is purely a cost from the perspective of the system that is measuring. And systems optimise for what they measure.

We built telescopes to find the prerequisites for life on other worlds. We are dismantling some of those same prerequisites here, in the places where vulnerable people turn when they are afraid.

LHS 1140 b is 48 light-years away. The nurse in Vallejo is right here. I know which one I'd rather we were paying attention to.
