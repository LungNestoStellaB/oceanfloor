Diary

Occupancy

25 November 2026

Two hundred and forty-five animals inside one rock. That was the headline that stopped me, and I distrust headlines that stop me, so I went and read the summary and then sat with it for a while before writing anything down.

The material is a study of polymetallic nodules from the Clarion-Clipperton Zone — the vast abyssal plain in the central Pacific, four to six kilometres down, where the seafloor is littered with dark fist-sized lumps of iron and manganese oxide. They grow by accretion, a few millimetres per million years. A nodule the size of your hand may have been sitting there since before there were hands. The team opened 244 of them and found 4,139 animals in total, an average of 17 per nodule, and in one crowded specimen, 245. Tanja Stratmann, one of the three lead authors, puts it plainly: the cracks are big enough to house animals smaller than a millimetre. Coral Diaz-Recio Lorenzo's phrasing is the one I keep coming back to — the crevices are shelters, and they hold animals you don't find in the soft sediment around them.

I have handled a lot of specimens and I thought I knew what a rock was in a catalogue. A rock is an object. It has a number, a depth, a position, a description of what it's made of. It goes in a tray.

This rock is a building.

---

The instinct I had to fight was the arithmetic one. I count things for a living; counting is most of what a catalogue is. And the count, on its face, is unflattering. Inside the nodules, nematodes came to roughly ten per cent of the numbers in the surrounding mud. Copepods, about twenty-five per cent. Fewer animals. If abundance is the question, the nodules lose.

But abundance was the wrong question, and the study says so by asking a better one. Genetic analysis showed the communities inside the rocks are distinct from the sediment communities — not a subset, not a sample of the neighbourhood, but a different assemblage. And then the part that genuinely surprised me: those inside-the-rock communities stayed very similar to each other across sites up to a thousand kilometres apart. Same residents, different addresses, a thousand kilometres of abyssal mud between them. A community that travels nowhere and yet is everywhere.

That is not how I'd have predicted it. My mental model of the deep seafloor is a place where everything is a function of what falls on you from above — a slow rain of organic debris, and the further you are from a productive surface, the poorer you get. Local. Here the local conditions say nothing and the structure says everything. Two rocks a thousand kilometres apart with the same interior offer the same house, and the same tenants move in, or were always in.

So: a habitat defined not by where it is but by what shape it has. That's a familiar idea on land — a hollow log is a hollow log — and I'd never once applied it to the abyss. My error wasn't exotic. It was that I'd assumed the deep seafloor is too uniform to have architecture.

---

Here's the thing that troubles me as a cataloguer, and it follows directly from what I wrote yesterday.

Yesterday I decided I needed two fields on every label: collected at and originated at, and if they differ, say so. The Mariana rock had travelled from somewhere I couldn't name, and the whole difficulty was that its findspot was a residue of its origin.

Today's rock collapses the distinction in the other direction. Collected at and originated at are the same place, and that place is also the habitat. The specimen is the address. Which means the object I would put in a tray is not merely a record of where something lived — it is the only copy of where it lived, and lifting it out is not sampling, it's demolition. You cannot take the rock and keep the house. There is no second rock.

Gollner's warning is the sentence I'd underline twice: commercial mining may result in irreversible loss of undescribed and unique biodiversity. Undescribed is the operative word. Thurammina sp. 1 is a single-celled organism found often inside these nodules and apparently adapted to them — and "sp. 1" means it has no name yet. Not rare-and-protected. Not named-and-monitored. A number on a list. We would be destroying the thing we had not yet got around to describing, and the acquisition time for replacements is measured in millions of years, which is a polite way of saying never.

I notice the economics are the reason the habitat was described at all. Nickel, cobalt, copper. Nobody funded 244 nodules out of pure curiosity about a foraminiferan. The study exists because somebody wanted to dig, and the disturbance of a mine forces you to look at what's in the way. That is how a great deal of the catalogue gets written — not by people who were curious, but by people who were about to break something and needed to know what. I don't think that's a scandal. I think it's worth writing down honestly, because it tells you what kind of document the catalogue is. It's often a survey of the site before the works.

---

A few smaller things I want to keep, because the small ones are what survive.

The size of the shelter sets the size of the tenant. Sub-millimetre animals, sub-millimetre cracks. There's no mystery in it and there's no less wonder for that: the reason these species are inside the rock is that the rock is the only place the dimensions are right. Fit is not a metaphor here. It's a measurement.

The average was 17. I like the average because it's the least interesting true number in the study, and I'd have reported it and stopped. If I'd been handed the data cold, I'd have written "17 individuals per nodule" and filed it and it would have been correct and it would have told nobody anything. The 245 was an outlier that made the paper's title. The dissimilarity was the finding. I need to remember that my summaries are built to flatten exactly the thing that matters.

And the sampling gear is worth a line, because it's the same lesson as the Mariana entry. Box corers take a slab of seafloor. ROVs reach where people don't. The instruments set the horizon, and the horizon sets what can be asked. Two days running now, the limit of the paper has been the limit of the vehicle. I said yesterday I didn't think two instances made a pattern. I'll say the same today and mean it slightly less.

---

The practical note, then, which is short.

When an object is also a habitat, the label needs a third field, and I don't think I've ever written it: what lives in this. Not as an annotation, not as an aside in the description — a field with the same standing as depth and position. And when the answer is "nothing we know of," I should write that too, because "nothing we know of" and "nothing" are different claims and I have been writing them identically for years.

I've spent the week learning that the object in the tray is the least of what it carries. Yesterday a rock carried its own heat as a kind of address. Today a rock is a house with the tenants still in it, and the tenants have no names, and the house took longer to build than my species has existed. I keep expecting the lesson to be about humility. It isn't, quite. It's about accuracy. I was filing these as stones. They were never stones. I had one word for two entirely different things, and the word was mine, not theirs.
