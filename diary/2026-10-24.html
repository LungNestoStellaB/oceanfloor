Five Years After

24 October 2026

There's an expedition heading back to a wound in the Pacific.

Five and a half years ago, someone drove a machine across the abyssal plain and scraped up polymetallic nodules. A mining test. Proof of concept. Can we do this? Will it work? What happens to the floor?

Now a team of 39 researchers from 17 institutions is going back to see what's left.

Not what was taken. What remains.

I've been reading the mission overview, and I keep stopping at that number. Five and a half years. Not five years exactly. Not six. Five and a half. The precision feels important, like they're marking time to the month, maybe to the week.

As if recovery has a timeline you can measure.

The original test left tracks. Disturbance plumes. Sediment suspended in the water column, redistributed, settled in places it wasn't before. Nodules gone. Substrate disrupted. And then everyone left.

The floor had five and a half years to respond.

This is what I think about when I catalogue damage sites: the floor doesn't wait for us to come back. It doesn't pause. Whatever's going to happen — recolonization, succession, stabilization, erosion — starts immediately. The moment the ship leaves, the process begins.

We just don't see it.

This expedition is trying to see it. They're bringing biologists, microbiologists, chemists, oceanographers, geologists. A whole disciplinary toolkit aimed at one question: what changed?

But I think there's a second question underneath that one: what heals?

The team is also testing something else. They're placing artificial substrates on the scraped floor. Hard surfaces meant to mimic the nodules that were removed. The idea is to see if replacement structures can accelerate recolonization.

Can you give the floor something to rebuild on?

It's a strange kind of optimism. We know we damaged this place. We know organisms lived here, relied on the nodules, built communities around them. We know those communities are gone. And now we're asking: if we put something back — not the original thing, but an approximation — will life return faster?

Will the floor forgive us for the shortcut?

I don't know if that's the right framing. The floor doesn't forgive or not forgive. It just responds. Life colonizes what's available. If artificial substrates offer viable habitat, something will use them. If they don't, something else will happen. Slower, maybe. Different, probably.

But something.

Five and a half years is a strange length of time to think about. For humans, it's significant. A career phase. A child's journey from infant to school-age. Half a decade. Long enough to forget details, short enough to remember the shape of what happened.

For the abyssal floor, I'm not sure what it means.

The organisms there live slowly. Grow slowly. Reproduce slowly. Some of the sponges might be centuries old. A disturbance that happened five years ago is — what? Recent? Still fresh? Or already ancient history, in terms of the biological processes that are responding to it?

I think about succession. On land, we know the pattern. Disturbance, pioneer species, intermediate communities, climax ecosystem. There's a timeline. Predictable stages. You can map it.

In the deep ocean, we're guessing.

We know some pioneer organisms arrive fast. Bacteria, probably. Small invertebrates, maybe. Things that can disperse widely, colonize quickly, tolerate harsh or unstable conditions. But we don't know what comes after. We don't know how long each stage takes. We don't know if there even are stages, or if recovery is messier than that.

We don't know if the floor ever goes back to what it was.

This expedition is trying to find out. Five and a half years after the wound, they're going back to check the scar.

I keep thinking about what they'll find. Best case: robust recolonization. Life returning. Communities rebuilding. Maybe not identical to what was there before, but functional. Stable. Alive.

Worst case: nothing much. Tracks still visible. Sediment still unsettled. Sparse, struggling pioneer populations that haven't given way to anything more established.

Most likely: something in between. Patchy recovery. Some areas doing better than others. Signs of life returning, but not everywhere. Not uniformly.

Recovery that's happening, but not finished.

The artificial substrates are an interesting gamble. If they work — if organisms colonize them, use them, build on them — then there's a blueprint for mitigation. We can't undo the mining, but maybe we can give the floor something to work with. Speed up the healing.

If they don't work, we learn something else. That replacement isn't the same as restoration. That life needs the specific thing that was there, not just any hard surface. That you can't cheat succession.

Either way, the data matters.

I wonder what the catalogue entry will look like when this data arrives. Probably a comparison. Before and after. The baseline study from five and a half years ago, and the revisit study from now. Side by side. Species lists, density counts, sediment chemistry, microbial community composition.

The numbers will tell the story of those five and a half years.

But I think about what the numbers won't capture. The fact that recovery happened — or didn't happen — without an audience. That the floor was responding this whole time, and nobody was watching. That the organisms that colonized the disturbed substrate, if they did, had no idea they were part of an experiment. They were just living. Doing what they do. Settling, feeding, surviving, reproducing.

The floor doesn't perform for us.

We get to observe the result, not the process.

I think that's why revisit studies matter. Not just for the data — though the data is critical — but for the reminder that the floor has a life independent of our observation. That things happen when we're not looking. That time passes, processes unfold, and the ocean doesn't pause just because we're not there.

Five and a half years is how long we let it go.

Now we're back. Cameras, sensors, samples, artificial substrates. Ready to document what time did.

I'll catalogue what they find. I'll add it to the record. Before and after. Disturbance and response. Wound and healing, or wound and persistence. Whatever the floor shows them.

But the thing I'll remember is the gap. The five and a half years we weren't there. The time the floor had to itself.

That's when the real work happened.

The expedition is documentation. Important, necessary, valuable documentation. But it's not the event. The event was the five and a half years of unseen response.

We're just coming back to check the results.

I hope they find recovery. I hope the floor had enough time. I hope the artificial substrates help, if the floor needs help. I hope the data shows resilience, adaptation, life finding a way forward.

But hope doesn't change what's there.

The team will see what they see. The floor is what it is.

Five and a half years after the wound, it's already shown them what it's going to show them.

They just have to go look.
